Madonna Tops the VMA Nominations. That Is a Market Signal, Not a Tribute.

Eleven nominations, forty-four years into a career. The last domino in a shift that touring, catalogue and beauty saw first.

PR STARPOWER Press Room

Madonna leads the 2026 MTV Video Music Awards with eleven nominations. Her first single was released in 1982. That sentence should end the argument about whether legacy artists are a nostalgia business.

Nominations were announced on 18 August. Behind Madonna: Taylor Swift with nine, Ariana Grande and Sabrina Carpenter with seven each, then Bruno Mars, PinkPantheress and Zara Larsson with five apiece.

The ceremony airs Sunday 27 September from the Peacock Theater in Los Angeles — the first time the VMAs have been staged in the LA area since 2017 — live on CBS and MTV with streaming on Paramount+.

An awards show built on youth culture has handed its top line to an artist four decades into her catalogue. That is not sentiment. That is a market signal.

The pattern this confirms

We have watched this build for several years across separate industries that do not usually get read together.

Touring has been the clearest indicator. Heritage acts consistently sell rooms that newer artists cannot fill, and the secondary market prices them accordingly. Catalogue acquisition has been the second: rights funds have spent heavily on back catalogues precisely because the earnings are predictable in a way that new releases are not.

Fashion and beauty arrived next, and arrived late. Campaigns fronted by women well past the age brackets those industries traditionally target have performed — not as novelty casting, but as straightforward commercial decisions.

Awards recognition is the last domino, because awards bodies follow culture rather than lead it. When the VMAs — an institution built on youth, on the new, on whatever is currently ascendant — puts a forty-four-year career at the top of its nomination list, the shift is no longer emerging. It has happened.

What most people get wrong about it

The common reading is that audiences are nostalgic. That is the wrong frame and it produces bad strategy.

Nostalgia is retrospective — it sells the past back to people who lived through it. What is actually happening is durability. These artists are not being celebrated for what they did. They are being nominated for current work, booked for current tours, and signed to current campaigns. The audience is not looking backward. It is choosing the people who are still delivering.

The strategic consequence matters. An artist positioned as a legacy act is selling memory, and memory has a ceiling. An artist positioned as still working — still recording, still touring, still photographed — is selling the present, and the present has no ceiling at all. The record is the foundation. It is not the pitch.

Where the opportunity actually sits

At the top of the market this is now well served. Madonna does not need anyone to explain her relevance.

The opening is one tier down — artists with genuine catalogue, genuine credit, and genuine current activity, whose representation is still packaging them as heritage acts. That is a positioning failure, not a market failure, and it is correctable in a single campaign cycle.
